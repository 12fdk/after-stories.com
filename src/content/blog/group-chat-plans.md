It starts with the good idea. Someone types *we should all get together soon*, the thumbs-up reactions come in, and for about forty-five seconds everyone is in. Then the group chat does what group chats do: the conversation branches, the date gets mentioned three times in three different forms, and by Thursday nobody can say whether it's Saturday or Sunday, and by Friday the plan is dead — not because anyone cancelled, but because the decision simply drifted.

This happens to every group I have ever been in. It is not a people problem. It is a format problem, and once you see the five ways a plan loses its oxygen, the fix is almost embarrassing.

## The five ways a plan dies in the group chat

**1. The decision scrolls away.** "So it's Saturday at 7 at Marco's?" "I think it was Sunday?" "Wait, who decides the food?" In a chat, a decision and a joke look identical once they are two hundred messages deep. Scrolling back for the answer costs effort, so nobody does it, and the group quietly re-plans from nothing.

**2. Open questions pile up.** Where, when, what's for eating, who's driving, does anyone have a reservation. Each one feels small. Together they are a list of things nobody has agreed on, and a plan with five open questions is five plans — every person is mentally holding a slightly different evening.

**3. One person quietly carries it.** The coordinator types eleven follow-up messages over three days, the group replies "sounds good" in a burst, and the coordinator is now the only person who knows where the plan actually stands. Carried plans collapse when the carrier has one bad day.

**4. The commitment is vague.** "When we go" is not a plan. "It's on" is not a plan. Vague commitments fail because nobody knows what would count as it being on — so nobody feels responsible for making it on.

**5. The window closes faster than the chat.** The idea and the logistics live on different timescales. The *we should do this* energy lasts a week; the group chat answers in days. If the decision takes four days, the energy is gone by day six, and the plan dies of age, not of disagreement.

## The fix: give the plan a spine

The pattern in every group plan that actually happens is the same: somewhere, someone kept the settled facts in one place and pushed the open questions one at a time. You can build that spine by hand. Here is the whole method.

### Step 1 — Split decided from undecided

At the moment a plan forms, write down the two lists out loud in the chat. Decided: it's a dinner, Saturday. Undecided: time, place, food. This single split changes the whole conversation, because now nobody is debating the whole evening — they are debating one item from a list that visibly shrinks.

### Step 2 — Let the group vote on the real choices

The choices the group actually cares about — which day, which place, which film — settle fastest as a vote, not a debate. A three-option vote closes in minutes. An open debate ("what if we went to that place but what if they have no tables...") circles for days. For anything the group has an opinion about, turn it into a vote.

### Step 3 — Assign an owner to each open question

"Who's checking the booking?" gets answered by everyone and by no one. "Ana, you grab the table by Wednesday" gets answered. One name, one task, one deadline. If a task has no owner, it is not a task — it is a hope.

### Step 4 — Share the place as a place, not an address

The moment a venue is chosen, share it properly: name, address, a map link you can open. Not "it's that Italian place near the station" — which is three messages of *which one, the one on which street, okay I'll look it up, got it* — but one thing everyone can open and trust. A venue shared as a place costs the group ten seconds. A venue shared as a description costs the group an argument.

### Step 5 — Define the go decision

Before the details are done, agree on what "it's on" means: "If nobody says no by Friday 9 pm, we're going." That one sentence converts the group's default (silent drift) into a decision. Nobody has to announce commitment; commitment is the default, and silence means go.

After Stories is built around exactly this spine — one plan gets one event, the group votes on the real choices, and a decision that's been made moves out of the chat and sits somewhere it can't scroll away. If the five steps above feel like too much to police manually, that is the gap it fills.

## What a plan that survives actually looks like

The groups that keep showing up share three habits, and none of them require a new app. The settled facts are in one place and never re-litigated. The undecided things are a short list that visibly shrinks, not a fog everyone carries. And the evening starts with the group knowing where they're going, what time, and who is bringing what — which is the entire difference between a plan that happens and a plan that almost happened.

## The one thing to do differently

Everything above collapses into a single habit: **don't let the decision stay in the conversation.** The moment the group agrees on something, move it out of the stream of messages into a place that is only for facts — and give every open question a name and a deadline. The group doesn't need to be more organised or more reliable; it just needs the decision to have somewhere to live. Do that, and the plan stops dying the way it always does.
