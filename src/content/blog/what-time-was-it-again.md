The plan is on. The enthusiasm is there, the day is picked, the group is all in. Then, two days later, someone asks the question that kills it: *so what time is it again?* Nobody answers. Nobody is sure. The place is fuzzy. Who is bringing what is a mystery. The plan is not cancelled — it is just unfindable, and an unfindable plan behaves like a plan that was never made.

This is the coordination problem, and it is almost always a storage problem, not a memory problem. The facts were decided. They just have nowhere to live.

## Why "what time was it again" has no answer

A group chat is a place where things are said, not where things are kept. When the group decided on Saturday at 7 at Marco's, that fact landed in the same stream as a joke, a photo of a dog, and a debate about the playlist. Fifty messages later, the decision and the joke look identical. To recover the time now, someone has to scroll — and scrolling is a task, so the group treats it as too much trouble and re-decides instead.

The deeper failure is that the group never separated the *settled* facts from the *open* conversation. In a running chat, both are just messages. There is no spot that means "this is true, this is current, this is the plan." So every question about the plan costs a scroll, and the cost quietly becomes higher than just planning a new evening.

The fix is not a stronger memory. The fix is giving the facts of the plan a place to live that is separate from the noise. Five steps do it.

## The five-step fix

### Step 1 — Decide the four things first, in one pass

Before the details, settle exactly four facts: the **day**, the **place**, the **time**, and the **go rule** — the moment after which silence means the plan is on. Do this in a single tight pass, not over three days. Once these four are down, write them out as one sentence: *Saturday, 7 pm, Marco's, on unless someone says no by Friday 9.* That sentence is the plan. Everything after it is logistics.

### Step 2 — Move the facts out of the chat

The moment the four facts are decided, pull them out of the running conversation and into a place that is *only* for facts. A pinned message, a note on a shared board, a single summary. The rule: the stream of messages is for talking; the pinned spot is for the plan. A fact that is decided should not be allowed to become just another message that scrolls away.

### Step 3 — Make each fact findable in one tap

A fact the group has to reconstruct is a fact that will get lost. The place should be shareable as a *place* — name, address, a map link anyone can open — not as the description "the Italian one near the station." The time should be a time, not "around seven-ish." The owner of each loose end should be a name, not "whoever." If answering any question about the plan takes more than a tap, the format is broken.

### Step 4 — Assign a name to every open question

The things that are not yet decided — food, transport, who brings what — each need an owner and a deadline. *Ana grabs the table by Wednesday. Marco confirms the time by Thursday.* A task with a name gets done. A task that belongs to "everyone" belongs to no one, and it becomes one of the things the plan quietly drifts on.

### Step 5 — Close with the go rule

Finish with the sentence that converts the plan into a default. *If nobody says no by Friday 9 pm, we are going.* This is the most important step and the one most groups skip, because it feels formal. It is the opposite of formal — it removes the work. Without it, silence means the plan might happen. With it, silence means it will.

After Stories exists because of exactly this: one plan gets one place where its facts live, the group votes on the real choices, and a decision that's been made moves out of the running chat so it can't scroll away. If step two feels like the part you always skip, that is the thing it makes automatic.

## The test that tells you the plan is solid

There is a simple test. Pick the person in the group who was least involved in the planning and ask them, cold: *where, when, and is it on?* If they answer in one second, the plan has a spine and it will survive. If they say "hmm, let me check the chat," the plan is still living in a conversation, and it will not survive.

## The one thing to do differently

**Stop treating the decision as something that was said, and start treating it as something that gets stored.** The group does not need to be more organised, more reliable, or more eager. It needs the facts of the plan to live somewhere that is not a scrolling message, and a single sentence — agreed up front — that says what silence means. Once the four facts have a home, the plan stops drifting, and the *what time was it again* question stops having no answer.
