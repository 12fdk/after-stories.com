The party itself runs fine. What goes wrong is the week around it: the date that kept getting re-asked, the venue whose last seating nobody checked, the cake with no one to collect it, and the bill that landed when everyone was already tired. A group birthday is about a dozen small facts, and most of them get buried in the middle of a conversation.

The fix is five decisions, made in an order where each one is still changeable.

## The birthday checklist in five steps

Decide these five, in this order, before the week starts. First, propose the date and the place yourself, check the venue's hours, and offer two options to the group. Second, pick the money format, split or separate tabs, and decide whether the birthday person pays, so it's settled before anyone orders. Third, send one invite with the date, the place, the money format, and a deadline for confirming.

Fourth, write down the small things the moment they're settled: the door code, who brings the cake, who drives, the playlist. Fifth, plan to look at the photos the morning after, while the night is still fresh. Money is easier to settle before the food arrives, which is why it comes second.

## Propose the date and the place

A date you propose yourself holds up better than one picked in a long thread, because there's a person who owns it. Before you invite anyone, check the venue's last seating, its capacity, whether you can bring a cake, and what its noise rules say. If the venue's availability rules out one weekend, offer two options to two or three close friends first and let them pick the one that fits; the rest of the group gets the chosen option through the invite below, not a second message.

## Settle the money before anyone orders

The two honest formats are splitting the shared bill between everyone present, or each person paying their own tab. Pick the split when everyone orders about the same amount. Pick separate tabs when one person is staying late or skipping a round. Decide the birthday person's share the same way: if the group covers it, say so in the invite and split the rest between everyone else; if they pay like everyone else, that goes in the invite too. Whichever format you pick, it goes in the invite.

## Invite once, with a deadline

The invite is one message that carries the date, the place, the money format, and a deadline for confirming. The deadline is the part that does the work: the last day a confirmation counts. A short version is, "confirm by Thursday or I'll take it for the plan." When the day passes, the list is what it is, and the plan moves on.

A group chat can carry the invite, but a chat also carries the memes and the 2 a.m. jokes, and the list scrolls away under all of it. After the invite goes out, the plan is each person's own to keep. If you want the plan itself to survive the week, keep it where it can be seen. We wrote about that split in [why plans die in the group chat](/blog/group-chat-plans/).

## Write down the small things

The facts that vanish are the ones decided in passing: the door code, who brings the cake, whose car holds the extra people, whose phone has the playlist. Whoever is organizing keeps a running list and adds to it the moment a fact is settled, one list on a phone, at the door if anyone needs it. The group doesn't all need to keep it. It needs to exist.

## Plan the morning after, while the night is fresh

The photos are what the group ends up keeping, and by default they live on a dozen phones. The night before, say one sentence: the photos go into one place, and we look at them tomorrow. That decision does most of the work, and it is the step that gets skipped. We wrote the full case for it in [the morning after](/blog/the-morning-after/).

That's the whole week: propose the date and the place, pick the money format, invite once with a deadline, write down the small things, and look at the photos the next morning. Get those five done before the week starts and the rest takes less pressure.
